Dear friend,

If someone in your family was in a car crash this year, you already know the part nobody warns you about. The crash takes one day. The bills take months.

They arrive out of order and out of nowhere. The ambulance ride. The imaging. The follow up visit. The rental car. The hours of work that quietly disappeared. And somewhere in the middle of that pile, an envelope from the insurance company arrives with a check in it.

Here is how it usually goes from there.

In one case our desk reviewed, the first check was $11,300. It sat on a kitchen counter with the bills stacked next to it, and it looked like relief. It was very nearly signed that same week, because when the bills are loud, any number sounds like quiet.

It was not signed. The claim got a full review first. Counted properly, with the treatment still ahead and the lost wages included, that claim ended near $118,000.

Every claim is different. Results vary, and no one can promise what any other claim may be worth.

The first check is not an insult. It is simply an accounting choice. It counts what has been billed so far, and not what is still coming. The release form stapled to it makes that early count permanent. That is the entire trick, and it is legal, and it works because signing feels like the responsible thing to do when a family is tired.

You would tell your sister to wait one more day and get the number checked. This letter is only here to tell you the same thing.
